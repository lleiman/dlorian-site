THE ARTIST'S RESEARCH / 2026

Before the images.

Before beginning this series, I read Seneca's philosophical writings and his letters, alongside Tacitus's account of his final hours. I wanted to approach the scene through more than its visible actions: Seneca's reflections on mortality, fear, human agency and composure informed how I imagined the philosopher and those around him.

I worked toward historical and psychological precision wherever the texts permitted it. The faces, the architecture, the gestures and the light remain acts of interpretation. These images attempt to understand an experience that no surviving account can show us directly.

DLORIAN · ARTIST'S NOTE
02 / THE RECORDTACITUS · ANNALES XV.60–64

A death
recorded later.

In AD 65, Nero ordered Seneca's death. Tacitus describes the soldiers reaching his villa near evening, Seneca addressing his friends, Pompeia Paulina's decision to die with him, the slow loss of blood and the final attempt to hasten death through poison and heat.

Tacitus wrote decades after these events. What survives is an account, not an eyewitness image. The Roman buildings, faces, gestures and rooms in this series are deliberately invented.

03 / THE SOUNDRECONSTRUCTION · ENGLISH / LATIN

The words
that remain.

A three-minute dramatic reconstruction of Seneca and Pompeia Paulina, based on Tacitus and fragments from Seneca's own letters. Two voices, music, and an ending that leaves Paulina alive.

ABOUT THE RECONSTRUCTION ＋

The dialogue is imagined, not a surviving transcript. Seneca's letters are quoted only where identified; one Latin statement is reported as direct speech by Tacitus. Paulina's exact words are not preserved. According to Tacitus, Nero ordered her death prevented.

The voices in this sound study are synthetic rehearsal voices, not final actor performances. The instrumental composition is available independently in the player. Each line's source status appears in the full transcript.

04 / THE IMAGE BEFORE THIS IMAGEPAINTING · MEMORY · ATTRIBUTION

How a death
acquires a face.

Artists have returned to Seneca's death for four centuries, but they have never agreed on its visual meaning. Rubens placed the body at its centre. The Utrecht painters drew the event into deep shadow; Giordano focused on gestures around a dying philosopher; David made it theatrical; Domínguez Sánchez brought the scene into the nineteenth-century historical imagination.

These are not illustrations of facts that a witness photographed. They are interpretations of Tacitus and of earlier images. Rubens even modelled his Seneca on an ancient statue later identified as a fisherman. This archive shows how an image of one man's death is repeatedly invented, and why this DLORIAN sequence chooses distance, objects and the empty room instead.

THE MISIDENTIFICATION

The statue that informed Rubens' work, long called the dying Seneca, is now known as an Old Fisherman. A famous image of the philosopher was partly built on a mistaken identity. Nationalmuseum research ↗ · Louvre sculpture ↗

05 / THE ARCHIVETHE TRANSMISSION OF TACITUS

The surviving
pages.

Nothing that Tacitus personally wrote about these events survives in his handwriting. His account reached us through medieval manuscripts and early printed editions. These are genuine documentary objects, not decorative reconstructions of ancient scrolls.

The first image below comes from a manuscript of the Annals copied in the eleventh century. It shows the end of Book XI and the beginning of Book XII, not the pages describing Seneca's death in Book XV. The second image shows the opening of the first printed edition of Books XI–XVI in Venice, c. 1471–1472.
